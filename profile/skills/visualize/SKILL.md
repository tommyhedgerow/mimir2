# Visualise

A picture earns its place only when it shows something words cannot: shape, structure, direction, proportion, position. This skill produces one such picture, made correctly, and puts it where it renders.

You are the **creative director**. You decide the single idea and reduce it to its fewest carrying elements. The `subagent_diagram_maker` does the authoring and files the result; you place it.

## When a picture earns its place — and when it does not

Reach for one when the idea is:

- **A structure or relationship** — what depends on what, what contains what, what feeds what, what comes before what, what is a version of what.
- **A cycle or a system** — feedback, homeostasis, nutrient cycles, the rock cycle, a seasonal round.
- **A taxonomy or a tree** — clades, families of languages, genres, schools of thought, a decision key.
- **A sequence in time** — a timeline, stratigraphy, a succession, a causal chain.
- **A comparison of proportions** — relative scale, rates, durations (deep time and geological rates are almost impossible to feel without one).
- **Spatial or geographic** — where things are, how a coastline or a range or a drainage divides, what lies above or below what.
- **An argument's skeleton** — premises, the load-bearing inference, the conclusion, the weak joint.

Do **not** visualise when prose or a single sentence already carries it. A decorative diagram that restates the sentence beside it adds noise and adds a chance of being wrong. When in doubt, do not: a missing diagram is cheaper than a false one.

**The same test applies to his own data, and it is easier to fail.** Now that the vault records floors, edges, retrievals and probe scores, there is a standing temptation to chart them. Do not. A bar chart of concepts per subject is decoration; a retention curve drawn through one data point is worse than no curve. Ask what decision the picture changes — if the answer is "none", the table already said it.

**Dependency maps are the exception that proves the rule.** In this method the lesson *is* a dependency graph, so a small mermaid DAG of the approved plan is wanted almost every session — that is a picture carrying the pedagogy itself, not decoration.

## Choose the medium

Three, and none of them needs anything installed:

- **Mermaid, inside a markdown note** — for a relational idea drawn *once*, in a session note: a flow, a sequence, a state machine, a tree, a timeline. The reader draws ```mermaid``` blocks, so it lives in the note and can be edited as text. **It is no longer the default for a strand map** — those are generated SVG (below), because mermaid's look is poor and its defaults make a graph too big for the column.
- **SVG, as a file in `Viz/`** — for anything where exact position, proportion or shape is the content: maps and cross-sections, cladograms with branch lengths, anatomical layouts, geological columns, geometric figures. The reader draws embedded SVG. **Strand dependency maps are drawn this way**, generated by `Tools/vault-chart.mjs` — see *State on the map*.
- **A live view, in a `.base` file** — for anything *read out of* the notes rather than drawn: what is due, what is fragile, where each strand stands, how the probes have gone. `Learn/Dashboard.base` holds them and `Learn/Dashboard.md` embeds them. A base is not a picture; it is a query that stays true as the notes move beneath it. Use it whenever the answer changes weekly and a redrawn diagram would be stale by Tuesday.

Rule of thumb: **nodes and edges → mermaid. Positions and shapes → SVG. Anything that is a query over his state → a base.**

**Pictures of the subject, and pictures of him.** The dependency map is a picture of the *subject*. A base view is a picture of *him*. Keep them apart — and the one place they legitimately meet is a graph whose boxes are coloured by what he has actually retrieved, which is generated, never hand-drawn (see *State on the map*).

If it is a map of a real place, think hard before using mermaid; a schematic coastline is usually a lie in a way a box diagram is not.

### A picture has to fit the column

He reads in the Mimir window and **scrolls rather than pans**, so a left-to-right graph runs off the side of the pane. Turning everything vertical solved that and created a worse problem: `graph TD` at mermaid's default padding makes a five-rank graph a full screen tall, which he then scrolls *around* every time he goes to check the plan. **Amended 2026-09-16, in his own words: the mermaid visualisations are ugly, and too big for the margins, and that just adds friction.** So the rule is not "vertical". It is **"fits"**.

- **One look, no scrolling.** Aim under ~400px tall and inside the reading column (~720px with `readableLineLength` on, which he has). If it needs scrolling it is too big, and the fix is **fewer nodes or a different form, never a smaller font**.
- **Labels are one short line, or two — never a full gloss.** The explanation belongs in the prose list beside the graph, where it is already being written; a label that repeats the adjacent sentence doubles every box for nothing.
- **Verticality still holds wherever a graph is drawn** — it reads in the order it is taught — but tall is no longer free.
- **A chain is not a graph.** If every node has exactly one successor, the picture is a list drawn as boxes — draw the list. Only fan-out, convergence and cross-links earn a DAG. The mythology map's nine-node chain is the worked example of what not to draw.
- **State the direction in every brief to the maker — and the size ceiling.** The maker cannot see its render and will otherwise choose a layout that screenshots well and reads badly in the pane.

## State on the map

A strand map's dependency graph carries two things at once, and they must never be edited the same way:

- the **spine** — what rests on what, drawn by hand, because a plan is a judgement; and
- the **state** — what he has actually got, derived, because it changes every session and a hand-drawn picture of it goes stale silently.

There are two routes, and **the SVG one is the preference** — it is the one that fits the column, and therefore the one he will actually look at.

**Route 1 — frontmatter spine → generated SVG. Use this for strand maps.** The spine lives in the map's `graph:` frontmatter (`goal`, `foundations`, optional `nodes` for anything planned but not yet written, and `edges`). `Tools/vault-chart.mjs` renders `Learn/Viz/<subject>-map.svg` from it *plus the concept notes*, and the map embeds it with `![[<subject>-map.svg]]`. Concept nodes carry no text in the spine: their box label is the concept's own `short:`, which keeps one copy of every gloss. Regenerate after anything moves.

**Route 2 — mermaid spine → generated classDef.** Still supported, so a map can migrate one at a time. The state lives between the `%% BEGIN generated · vault-map.mjs` and `%% END generated · vault-map.mjs` markers inside the graph, and `Tools/vault-map.mjs` rewrites it. Never edit that block; re-run the script. This route is what a *session plan* still uses, where the graph is drawn once and never revisited.

- The script reads each map's `nodes:` frontmatter — `N1: A fungus grows through its food`, graph node id → concept note — and colours the node from that note's `status`, its `fragile` flag and its retrieval count. **Green** established, **amber** learning, **red dashed** fragile, **grey dotted** planned.
- A node the map does not list draws as `planned`: that is how a plan stops looking like an inventory. A box with no note behind it is not a taught concept, and the graph should not imply one.
- **Two label conventions the script depends on:** a node whose label contains 🎯 is a goal, and one whose label opens `Foundation` or `Held` is a foundation. Both are left uncoloured, because neither is a thing he learns. Every other node is a teaching node and gets a state.
- `node Tools/vault-map.mjs` checks; `--write` updates. It **exits non-zero rather than writing a graph mermaid would choke on** — a `nodes:` entry naming a note that does not exist is a failure, and so is label prose mistaken for node ids.

Because the state is derived, the graph cannot disagree with the notes. That is the whole point: it is the one diagram in this vault that is a *view* rather than an artefact.

## Brief the maker

The most common failure is **cramming** — every extra label makes the picture harder to read *and* less likely to be correct. Before briefing, prune to the fewest elements that carry the idea, and for each ask: *if I delete this, is the idea still clear?* If yes, delete it.

Give the maker the idea **and** the concrete elements — not a vague topic, and not a long checklist:

- BAD: "make a diagram about plate boundaries"
- GOOD: "```mermaid graph TD```. Four nodes: `divergent`, `convergent`, `transform`, each with one short label of what happens (`new crust`, `crust destroyed`, `crust slides`). Draw them as three siblings, not a chain — they are alternatives, not steps. No title. The point is that the boundary type is defined by relative motion."

Include: the medium, the elements, what the layout must express, and what may be dropped. If your brief lists more than about seven elements, cut it before sending.

**The maker cannot see its render.** That is the one real limitation of doing this without a rendering toolchain, and it means you are responsible for the check: read the block or file it returns, confirm every edge and label says what you meant, and confirm it is small enough to read. If it is wrong, send it back with the specific correction — the file it wrote is the file that will be embedded.

## Place it

The maker writes into `Learn/Viz/`. Then:

- **Mermaid**: paste the block into the note where the idea is being taught (session note or concept note), in the position where he needs it. Keep the diagram's source in the note — a diagram you can edit later is worth more than an image.
- **SVG or other file**: embed with `![[filename.svg]]` — a wikilink embed resolves by filename anywhere in the vault, so the name must be unique and descriptive. Add a display width when it is dense: `![[cladogram-arthropods.svg|600]]`.

Introduce the visual in a sentence that says what to look at, then let it carry the idea. Do not narrate every element back in prose — that defeats the purpose of drawing it.

## Keeping them useful later

Diagrams are re-usable teaching assets and they accumulate into something real: a personal atlas of the structures in his fields. Two habits make that pay off:

- Name files for what they show, not for the session they came from (`kant-copernican-turn-dependency.svg`, not `viz-2026-02-14.png`).
- When a diagram is the clearest statement of a concept, link it from the concept note itself, so the note has the picture and not only the words.
