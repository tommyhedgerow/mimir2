# Humanities playbooks

The two principles do not change; what counts as an unconditional truth does. In these subjects clean universal statements are rare and usually false, and the sound foundation is almost always a **definition, a distinction, a problem, or a constraint** rather than a law. Find one of those and the method works exactly as advertised. Pretend a universal law exists where the field has none and you teach him something the first real book will demolish.

Common shape for the foundation layer across all four: *the problem the field was invented to solve*, and *the distinction that makes the problem visible*. That is usually the ground he can take at face value.

## Philosophy

**Foundations are usually definitions and distinctions**, and the discipline is largely made of them: the difference between validity and truth, between a reason and a cause, between descriptive and normative, between knowledge and true belief. Those are not facts to memorise, they are tools, and a definition is only really held when he can use it to sort a case he has not seen.

- **Teach the problem, not the position.** Every philosophy is an answer to something. Start from the puzzle that made the answer necessary — the regress, the sceptical scenario, the seeming conflict between two things he already believes — and let him feel the pressure before you name the position. This is Principle ii in its purest form, and it is what makes the history of philosophy a sequence of moves rather than a list of names.
- **The unit of work is the argument.** For any position: state its strongest form (steelman it — use `subagent_sophist` if you are unsure), identify what it rests on, then find the objection that costs it most. Ask him to make the objection before you supply one.
- **Check with cases, not labels.** "Is this argument valid?" on a new argument beats "what is validity?". Being able to name a fallacy is not being able to spot one.
- **Dates and attributions are the trap.** "Plato said", "Aristotle argued", "Kant's Copernican turn" are historical claims: check the locus, not just the idea. Distinguish what an author wrote from what a tradition made of it, and be suspicious of any tidy ism.
- **The trap of the survey.** A beginner's "history of philosophy" taught as a march of positions produces a pile of disconnected labels — the exact failure the method exists to prevent. Choose depth over coverage and say what you are leaving out.

## History

**Foundations here are constraints and scales**, not laws: chronology is a hard constraint (a claim needing a text written after its author died is dead), geography and climate bound what was possible, population and energy bound what could be built, and the sources that survive determine what can be known at all. Teach those and cause-and-effect becomes derivable instead of arbitrary.

- **The central distinction to install early: evidence and inference.** A source is not the past; it is a trace, and history is the disciplined argument from trace to past. Once he holds that, "how do we know?" becomes a real question with real answers, and the subject stops feeling like a list of things that happened.
- **Always ask the source's four questions**: who made it, when, for whom, and what could they have known? A chronicle written for a patron, a tax record, a letter, a coin and a midden are different kinds of evidence with different failure modes.
- **Absence of evidence is evidence only when the record would have recorded it.** This single rule prevents most bad historical reasoning, including a lot of his own.
- **Teach from the inside of a decision.** What did the actors know at the time, what did they want, what did they think was possible? Hindsight makes everything look inevitable; the past was not. This is where the "how could I have discovered this" move lands hardest.
- **Periodisation is a convention, not a fact.** "The Middle Ages", "the Renaissance", "the Industrial Revolution" are tools with edges that leak. Say what a periodisation is for and where it breaks.
- **Expect the myths**: the flat-earth Middle Ages, the lone genius, the tidy progress narrative, the single cause, the nation as an ancient natural unit. When a story has that satisfying shape, check it.

## Literature

**Foundations are the text and the conventions of reading.** Literature has no laws either; what it has is a text that constrains interpretation, and a set of conventions (genre, metre, form, narrative voice, figurative language) that are learnable and reusable. Those conventions are the graph's nodes: once he holds "what an unreliable narrator is", a hundred texts become readable through it.

- **Close reading is a skill, so it must be practised, not described.** Put two lines in front of him and ask what the choice of *that* word does that a near-synonym would not. Do not narrate the interpretation; let him find the evidence, then name what he found.
- **Interpretation is answerable to the text.** Rival readings must be stated as readings, with the evidence each leans on. "Any reading is valid" is a misconception worth dislodging explicitly and early.
- **Context earns its place when it changes the reading** — an unfamiliar convention, a word's period sense, a genre expectation the author is playing against. Context delivered as background is the least efficient teaching there is; context delivered as the answer to a puzzle in the text sticks.
- **Quotations must be exact and located.** Edition, act, line, book. Smoothing a quotation from memory is a real error and he will notice.
- **Translation is a decision.** When a passage turns on a word, compare two translations and ask which choice does what. That single move teaches more about how meaning works than any amount of commentary.

## Language as culture: etymology, names, and words

Where the subject is language itself rather than a language skill:

- **Etymology is the minefield of confident nonsense.** Folk etymologies are *designed* to feel right. Check every one, and whenever you can, give the actual reconstructed path with its uncertainty marked — the uncertainty is part of the lesson, and it inoculates him against the genre.
- **Sound change is regular; meaning change is not.** That contrast is an unconditional truth of the field, and it explains why cognates can look unrelated and why a word's current sense tells you little about its history.
- **Names carry ideology.** Place names, species names, and period names record who named them and why. That is a durable insight, and it links straight into history, botany and geography.
- **Never invent a language fact** — a cognate, a root, a first attestation, a grammatical term. These are exactly the kind of plausible-looking detail that gets fabricated. Verify with the researcher or a dictionary that gives dates.

## What a good humanities session looks like

Foundations of that kind (a definition, a distinction, the problem); then the discovery path through a *specific* case — one argument, one decision, one passage, one word — with him doing the reasoning; then a check that requires applying the tool to a case he has not seen; then the concept note, whose "why it has to be this way" section is where the argument's skeleton is preserved for review. One case taught properly beats four surveyed.
